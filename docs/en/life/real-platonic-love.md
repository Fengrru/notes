A lot of people say that Platonic love means talking only about the soul and never about the body.

Love of the spirit.

Pure love.

Some even believe that as long as there's no physical relationship, this love is somehow higher than ordinary love.

But do you know?

That is precisely the modern world's biggest misunderstanding of Plato.

Because Plato never told you that true love means doing without the body.

He talked about love because he wanted to know something far more troublesome.

Why do people love beauty?

Why does the mere existence of another person make someone start asking who they themselves are?

Why does love make an ordinary person suddenly start thinking about truth, goodness, beauty, knowledge — even the truth itself?

A friend asked me:

“So what is real Platonic love, actually?”

I said:

“First tell me — what do you think it means to love someone?”

He said:

“Liking her, understanding her, wanting to be with her.”

I asked:

“And if she doesn't want to be with you?”

He froze for a moment.

“Then I let it go.”

“Why?”

“Because she doesn't like me.”

I smiled.

“So what you love — is it her, or the feeling she gives you when she likes you?”

He said nothing.

I asked again:

“If a person no longer matches your imagination of her, do you still love her?”

“If she's no longer gentle, no longer sensible, no longer replying the second you text, no longer worshipping you — if she even starts becoming completely different from you.”

“Would you still say she's your soulmate?”

He was silent for a long time.

“Probably not.”

I said:

“That's the problem.”

What many people call a meeting of souls is really just the other person happening to fit the script they wrote.

But isn't Platonic love just love of the spirit?

My friend asked.

No — at least, not only that.

What Plato really discusses is how love begins as obsession with one person and gradually moves toward the pursuit of beauty, the soul, knowledge, and even truth.

That doesn't sound like being in love.

My friend said.

Because you understand love too narrowly.

You think love is two people holding each other.

What Plato wanted to ask is what happens to a person's inner world after they fall in love with someone else.

At first you might fall in love with a person because of her face, then stay because of her mind, and later you begin to love that unique way of being alive in her.

In the end, because you met her, you start thinking for the first time about what beauty is, what goodness is, and what kind of life you truly want.

She may only be the starting point of love.

But she opened a door for you.

The world behind that door is vaster than she is.

My friend suddenly laughed.

“Then the way you put it, Platonic love is pretty romantic.”

I shook my head.

“Quite the opposite.”

It's actually far crueler.

Because it asks you to understand something modern people find hardest to accept.

What?

Loving someone is not the same as owning them.

You can love her deeply, and she still belongs to herself.

You can hope she stays, but you must accept that she's free to leave.

You can want to be near her, but you can't, out of love, decree what she should become.

This is where love truly becomes difficult.

But many people today say they want Platonic love.

They don't want a physical relationship.

That's fine, of course.

The problem is that some mistake “no physical relationship” for “the spirit must be superior.”

As if, simply by not mentioning the body, you automatically possess a soul.

But a person can never touch your body and still possess your emotions.

They can say they respect your freedom while demanding to know why you didn't reply.

They can claim to respect your boundaries while getting angry that you don't live up to their expectations.

What do you call that?

My friend asked.

I said:

“Spiritual possession.”

Humans see physical desire as low, yet rarely realize that controlling someone's thoughts, emotions, and choices can be more possessive than touching their body.

I said:

“True Platonic love is not the absence of desire.”

“It's that you have desire, and yet you don't turn that desire into a right.”

You want her to stay, but you don't use love as a rope.

You want to change her, but in the end you accept her right to become someone you can't understand.

My friend asked:

“So does that mean the highest form of loving someone is letting go?”

I said:

“No.”

Then what is it?

It's that you don't need to let go in order to prove how noble you are.

If she stays, you respect her.

If she leaves, you respect her too.

If she loves you, you cherish it.

If she doesn't, you accept it.

You own your feelings, but you don't turn them into the other person's obligation.

I looked at him:

“True freedom was never 'I do whatever I want.'”

“It's that I can have desire, without letting desire make my decisions for me.”

Later my friend asked:

“Then why do so many people misunderstand Plato?”

I said:

“Because humans especially love to give their feelings a name that sounds superior.”

Not getting it, they call fate.

Not being able to forget, they call deep affection.

Coldness, they call clarity.

Avoidance, they call having boundaries.

Being afraid to love, they call independence.

Fantasy, they call a soulmate.

Sometimes it's not that we don't want to love — we're just afraid of someone too real.

So we'd rather love an imagination that will never disappoint us.

My friend asked:

“So you think Platonic love is fake?”

I said:

“No.”

I just think real Platonic love is far harder than we imagine.

It's not pulling love out of the body; it's putting a person's dignity back into love.

It's not demanding the other be like you, but allowing them to become someone different from you.

It's not trapping her inside your imagination, but seeing her as she truly is and still being willing to understand.

He said:

“Then what if one day I find that the person I love is completely different from what I imagined?”

I said:

“Then you've only just begun to truly love her.”

Why?

Because before that, you were in love with your own imagination.

Only after that do you have a chance to meet her.

I suddenly remembered a line:

What a person most easily falls in love with is never another person, but the part of themselves that another person happens to satisfy.

But the real question was never:

“Is she my other half?”

It's:

“When she no longer matches my imagination, am I still willing to admit that she is still a whole person?”

If the answer is no.

Then what you loved was never her.

You only fell in love with an obedient illusion.

And this may be the cruelest truth of “Platonic love”:

True love of the soul is never turning two people into one; it is two whole people who can draw close to each other, yet never lose the right to be themselves.

So in the end I finally understood.

What we truly long for may never have been a “soulmate.”

We are simply desperate to meet one person:

who can see through us, yet never tries to remake us;

who is willing to come close, yet never possesses us;

who loves us, yet lets us keep the part of ourselves he can't understand.

And if such a person truly exists…

you will find.

The deepest love never made you into his other half.

It only let you, in another person's gaze,

see yourself, whole, for the first time.

And then one day, you each walk toward your own distance.

You don't hate her.

And she owes you nothing.

You just suddenly understand:

Some people appear in your life not to become your destination.

They only let you know, for the first time, that once one soul has been lit by another, even after that person leaves, the light doesn't go out right away.

This may be the longest riddle Plato left to love:

Are we loving a person — or, through loving a person, slowly learning how to love the world?
