## The Question Behind the Question

Ask someone what they think of evaluation systems — exams, rankings, KPIs, citation counts — and you'll usually get a complaint about pressure or a defense of fairness. Both miss the real issue.

The real issue is this: **most human suffering around evaluation doesn't come from losing. It comes from winning a game you never wanted to play.**

This post is my attempt to think that through — from how these systems work, to why they hurt, to how you take the scoring power back.

## Every Metric Is a Compression Algorithm

Start with a cold, structural fact: every standardized evaluation system in society — test scores, degrees, titles, performance reviews, follower counts — is a **low-cost information compression mechanism**.

A school cannot deeply understand every student's thinking ability, creativity, persistence, and interests. So it uses a score as a *proxy signal*. A company cannot observe every employee's actual contribution, so it uses KPIs. Large-scale social cooperation genuinely requires standardized signals; without them, nothing coordinates. In a country with enormous population competing for limited seats, an exam score is arguably the *least-bad* option — it strips out connections and family background and gives everyone one hard, legible currency.

So far, so reasonable. The system isn't evil. It's a dashboard.

The corruption happens at a precise moment, and it has a name: **Goodhart's Law**. *When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.*

Watch the substitution happen in slow motion:

- Original logic: real goal (learn → understand → grow) → metric (score) as an *observation signal*
- Alienated logic: metric → study *for* the metric → the real goal quietly discarded

"I want to score well" mutates into "a high score proves I am a valuable person." The proxy has eaten the target. Teachers cram for evaluations, students grind mock exams year-round, employees fabricate numbers — and the score stops being a health checkup and becomes a verdict on your worth as a human being. The humiliation of dropping ten ranks in one monthly exam outweighs everything the exam could have diagnosed.

That's the source of the pain. Not competition itself — **goal substitution**.

## The Optimization Function You Never Chose

Here's a framing that clarified everything for me: every person is running a *life optimization function*, whether they know it or not.

For most people, the function was pre-installed by the environment:

> maximize( social approval, income, ranking, status )

They never wrote it. They never audited it. They just execute it — and then wonder why winning feels hollow. Because even when the game is won, it was never *their* game. A person built for creating, forced to chase standard answers. A person built for long-term exploration, forced to chase short-term numbers. Victory in someone else's function still leaves your own variables at zero.

Other people run a different function:

> maximize( value created, unknowns explored, people helped, proximity to the problems I actually care about )

Same world, same 24 hours — completely different behavior, completely different relationship with external metrics.

Consider a researcher. The external system measures paper count, venue rank, citations, titles. The internal system asks: *did I propose a genuinely new idea? Did I move the field? Did anyone benefit from my tool? Am I getting closer to the problem I actually want to work on?* Both systems exist. The people I admire don't reject the external one — they **use it as a tool, never as a steering wheel.**

## Open Source as a Counter-Example

The clearest proof that "the scoreboard" is a design choice, not a law of nature, is the open source community.

The traditional system scores you on exams, school rank, credentials. The open source world scores you on code quality, problems actually solved, users served, whether other people *choose* to build on your work. Both are external evaluation systems — but the second one sits far closer to real contribution.

And history is full of people who were mediocre in the traditional system but compounded relentlessly in the one they chose: no elite degree, but a library used by tens of thousands; no title, but a tool that changed how people work. Those are grades too. Just from a different school.

## The Trap of a Purely Internal System

But here's the failure mode nobody warns you about, and it's important enough to be honest about: **building your own evaluation system is not the same as ignoring all external feedback.**

A purely internal system drifts, predictably, into self-protection. It's very easy to design a scoreboard where you always win: *"I don't care what anyone thinks; I know I'm brilliant"* — while producing nothing of value to anyone. That's not sovereignty. That's a closed loop of self-deception.

The healthy architecture is a **dual-track system**, with the responsibilities strictly separated:

**The internal system holds the steering wheel.** It answers *who do I want to become?* Its metrics: Am I closer to my ideal? Am I still curious? Did I do real thinking today? Did I create and ship anything? It decides what matters and what isn't worth your energy.

**The external system is a calibration instrument — with no decision-making power.** It answers *is my work actually landing in reality?* Its metrics: user feedback, community response, collaborators' honest assessments, concrete outcomes. External signals are *information*, not sentences. They correct your blind spots; they don't get to rewrite your direction.

The correct wiring: **internal decides where to go; external reports how the road is actually going.**
The broken wiring, which is most people's default: external decides where to go; internal feelings get sacrificed indefinitely.

## What This Means When You're Inside the Machine

None of this is a license to opt out. If you're in high school — or in any system where a score is the entry ticket — the score is still society's accepted currency. The framework doesn't say "stop caring." It says: **be tactically serious and strategically dismissive.**

Treat the score like a health bar in a game. When it drops, it means one thing: this knowledge point isn't trained yet. Patch it. It does not mean *you* are broken. A bad result is a diagnostic printout, not a character judgment.

And learn to distinguish two kinds of effort: energy spent *using* the system to earn your entry ticket and expand your future options — versus energy burned in meaningless over-optimization, chasing metrics that mean nothing to you, at the cost of the exploration and building that actually compound into who you become. The school cares about its admission rate. You care about your entire life. Those are not the same optimization function, and only one of them is your job.

The two systems even divide the timeline cleanly: the external one handles *selection* — it decides which door opens next. The internal one handles *formation* — it decides who walks through. Three years of noise versus a lifetime of foundation.

## The Psychology of De-Weighting

Owning your evaluation system isn't just an intellectual move — it's a psychological discipline. A few operating principles, stripped of any cinematic extremism:

**Confidence is de-weighting, not self-praise.** Genuine confidence doesn't come from repeating "I'm great." It comes from deliberately lowering the weight that others' judgments, social norms, and rejection hold inside your head. Not out of arrogance — out of a refusal to let an external scoring system define self-worth. Criticism can't edit a self-definition it was never given write-access to.

**Separate the failure from the person.** Most inferiority comes from equating "this attempt failed" with "I am a failure." Keep the ledger clean: actions can fail; personal worth is not contaminated by it. Failure is an objective event, not a verdict. The moment you grant yourself permission to mess up, the fear that blocks all action dissolves.

**Your identity needs no external props.** A self built on clothes, titles, gear, and other people's approval is permanently fragile — every prop is a hostage. Strip away all the socially-assigned labels; whatever still stands and says *this is what I'm building* — that's the actual you.

**Drop likability as a survival strategy.** Most people instinctively prune their real thoughts to fit others' expectations. Being direct about what you want and think isn't rudeness — it's demoting "being liked by everyone" from primary objective to pleasant side effect. Loyalty to your own volition first; diplomacy second.

**Generate meaning; don't wait to receive it.** When society's prescribed goals stop convincing you, the anxiety isn't a malfunction — it's an invitation. The world ships with no built-in meaning. Meaning is self-assigned, and confidence built on self-assigned purpose can't be repossessed by anyone.

**Act first; the mindset follows.** The common belief is that confidence must precede action. The causality runs backwards: confidence is *forged* by repeated action and repeated exposure to uncertainty. Don't wait for the perfect mental state. Let behavior reshape the mind.

One boundary worth stating plainly: strong subjectivity is not antisocial. Ignoring the *weight* of external judgment is not the same as ignoring law, ethics, or other people's wellbeing. We're taking the internal valuation system — nothing else.

## Three Questions for the Audit

If you want something operational, here it is. Periodically — monthly is enough — run these three questions against whatever is consuming most of your time:

1. **Is this serving a goal I defined, or just inflating an external proxy metric?**
2. **If nobody ever saw this and there were no reward, would I still do it?**
3. **Of the external feedback I'm receiving, which parts are objective calibration — and which are just other people's value preferences that I'm free to decline?**

The first question catches goal substitution. The second finds what you actually value. The third keeps the calibration channel open without handing over the steering wheel.

## Evaluation Sovereignty

Here's where all of it converges.

Maturity, in this domain, is not rejecting society's rulers or pretending metrics don't exist. It's a quieter, harder thing: **I know exactly what yardsticks society measures me with — and I decide which of them enter my life's optimization function, which serve as mere reference, and which get discarded entirely.**

That's not escapism. It's the difference between being a passive variable inside someone else's evaluation system and being the architect of your own.

Every system will hand you a scoreboard. School hands you a rank. Work hands you a KPI. The internet hands you a follower count. You can't stop them from keeping score. But whether their numbers are *your* numbers — that has always been, and remains, your call.

Refuse to win games you don't want to play. Design the one you do.
